Field notes

What we keep seeing inside hospitals.

Short pieces from working on real claims desks. No theory, no vendor language.

A TPA query costs far more than the reply

Queries

Writing the answer to a query takes about ten minutes. That is never where the cost is. The cost is the three days spent finding out which claim the query belongs to, digging the file out, working out which doctor can answer it, and then catching that doctor between operations.

Meanwhile the fifteen-day window is running. Most claims that die on a query do not die because the hospital had no answer. They die because the answer arrived on day sixteen, or because nobody noticed the clock at all. The fix is not a better reply. It is knowing on day one exactly what is being asked, of whom, and how long is left.

Claims fail on paperwork, not on medicine

Rejections

Look at a month of rejected claims in almost any hospital and the pattern is the same. An unsigned page. A discharge summary that never made it into the file. A high-end antibiotic billed against a simple fever code. A final bill that quietly crossed the approved amount two days before discharge.

Almost none of it is a clinical dispute. The treatment was correct and the patient went home well. The money was lost afterwards, in a folder. That is worth sitting with, because it means the recovery is an administrative problem with an administrative fix, not a negotiation with the insurer.

What the national claims exchange changes, and what it does not

NHCX

The exchange is a real improvement. One route to many payers instead of a separate portal, login and format for each. Claims move faster and their status comes back in a form a system can read.

What it does not do is check your claim. If the file that goes out is incomplete, the exchange delivers that incomplete file faster and the rejection arrives sooner. Being connected is not the same as being ready. The work that decides whether you get paid still happens inside the hospital, before anything is sent.
